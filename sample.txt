""  Brief on Cases Concerning Police Officers’ Immunity
Police officers’ immunity is a legal protection that may shield officers from personal liability or prosecution for certain acts performed in the course of their duties. However, immunity is not absolute: its scope depends on the jurisdiction, the type of proceedings, and the circumstances of the officer’s conduct.
The main legal issues include:
- Qualified or functional immunity: Whether an officer can be held personally liable for unlawful conduct while carrying out official duties.
- Use of force: Whether force used during an arrest or detention was lawful and proportionate.
- Arrest and detention: Whether an officer is protected when acting without lawful authority.
- Criminal liability: Whether official status protects an officer from prosecution for an offence.
- Human rights violations: Whether immunity can prevent accountability for violations such as torture, unlawful killing, or arbitrary detention.
Important cases on police immunity in the UK
1. Hill v Chief Constable of West Yorkshire [1989] AC 53
The case concerned the police investigation into the Yorkshire Ripper murders. The House of Lords held that the police did not owe the claimant a duty of care in negligence in relation to the investigation and prevention of crime in the circumstances.
Principle: Police are not automatically liable in negligence for failing to prevent crime. This is not a blanket immunity from all legal claims.



2. Robinson v Chief Constable of West Yorkshire Police [2018] UKSC 4
An elderly woman was injured when police officers attempted to arrest a suspect in a public street. The Supreme Court rejected the argument that the police enjoyed a general immunity from negligence claims.
Principle: Police officers can be liable for injuries caused by their negligent acts, just like other people, subject to ordinary legal principles.

3. Michael v Chief Constable of South Wales Police [2015] UKSC 2
Joanna Michael called the police after her former partner threatened to kill her. A delayed response meant officers did not arrive before she was murdered.
Principle: The Supreme Court held that the police did not owe her estate a common-law duty of care on the facts. The case illustrates the distinction between failing to protect someone from harm and actively causing harm.

4. DSD and NBV v Commissioner of Police of the Metropolis [2018] UKSC 11
The case involved serious police failures in investigating sexual offences committed by John Worboys.
Principle: The police can be held responsible under Article 3 of the European Convention on Human Rights for serious failures to investigate credible allegations of serious violence. Police immunity does not automatically defeat human-rights claims.

5. Tindall v Chief Constable of Thames Valley Police [2024] UKSC 33
The case concerned whether police intervention and their subsequent failure to protect a person from danger could give rise to negligence liability.
Principle: The Supreme Court reaffirmed that there is generally no duty to protect someone from harm caused by others, unless an established exception applies. The decision concerns the existence of a duty of care rather than blanket immunity for police officers.

Conclusion
The central lesson from these cases is that police officers do not have absolute immunity from civil liability or human-rights claims in the UK. Courts distinguish between negligent acts, failures to prevent harm, failures to investigate crime, and violations of fundamental rights.
For a legal essay, Hill, Robinson, Michael, DSD and Tindall provide a useful starting point.
